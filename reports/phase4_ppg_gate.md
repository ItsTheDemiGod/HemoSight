## 1. GATE A - does the AC/DC normalisation cancel? **FAIL**

> **FAIL**. The normalisation does not reduce non-haemoglobin variance on real data. It *increases* it: median nuisance CV goes from **0.563** for raw DC to **1.530** for AC/DC (-171.6%) and **1.115** for the ratio-of-ratios (-98.0%). The declared threshold was a reduction of 50% or more.

| feature group | median nuisance CV | reduction vs raw DC | median R^2 with Hb |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| RAW DC (carries every nuisance) | 0.5633 | - | 0.0037 |
| RAW AC | 1.1461 | -103.5% | 0.0006 |
| NORMALISED AC/DC | 1.5299 | -171.6% | 0.0024 |
| RATIO-OF-RATIOS R | 1.1152 | -98.0% | 0.0032 |

The best single feature has an equivalent haemoglobin noise of **9.75 g/dL** against a population spread of only **1.470 g/dL**. The noise is 6.6x the signal it has to resolve.

### An honest limitation of Gate A as designed

Part of the cancellation claim is **not testable on this dataset**. AC/DC is supposed to cancel source intensity and sensor gain - but all 252 subjects were recorded on **one device**, so there is no device variation present for it to cancel. The between-subject DC variation here (CV 0.39-0.63) reflects finger thickness, perfusion and skin tone, not instrument differences.

What Gate A *does* establish is the part that matters: AC/DC also claims to cancel **static tissue absorption**, and it demonstrably does not - the normalised features are more subject-variable than the raw DC they came from, and none of them carries haemoglobin information. So the gate is weak on one half of its premise and decisive on the other.

**A phone sees one of the four wavelengths, in one of its three channels.** Three quarters of the dataset's measurement is invisible to the target hardware. That is a hardware fact, independent of any model.

## 3. Task 3 - the baselines, and why they decide the phase

| model | MAE g/dL | Pearson r | R^2 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| population mean | 1.175 | +0.000 | +0.000 |
| sex alone | 0.831 | +0.644 | +0.415 |
| demographics (age, sex, height, weight) | 0.831 | +0.642 | +0.411 |

Two things follow, and both matter more than any PPG number:

1. **Sex alone (MAE 0.831) equals full demographics (0.831).** Age, height and weight add nothing. This is ordinary physiology - men carry higher haemoglobin, which is why the WHO thresholds themselves differ by sex (13 g/dL for men, 12 for women).
2. **Adding PPG to demographics does not improve demographics.** Demographics alone 0.831; four-wavelength PPG plus demographics 0.824; phone-RGB plus demographics 0.824. The differences are noise.

> **This is the trap the pre-declared thresholds would have walked into.** A 'PPG + demographics' model scores MAE 0.824, which is inside the VIABLE band. Reported without the baseline it would look like a working PPG haemoglobin estimator. It is a sex classifier with a PPG-shaped decoration attached. Task 3 exists precisely to catch this, and it did.

## 4. Task 2 - signal quality and abstention

| SQI threshold | rejection rate |
| --- | --- |
| SQI < 0.1 | 4.4% |
| SQI < 0.2 | 10.7% |
| SQI < 0.3 | 17.5% |
| SQI < 0.5 | 32.1% |

**It does not repeat the Phase 2 defect.** The Phase 2 image quality score had AUROC 0.816 yet rejected 0 of 17 deliberately-bad frames - it ranked but never refused. This SQI rejects **17.5%** at its operating threshold, and the rejected group does have higher error (0.930 vs 0.810 g/dL).

But the relationship is weak and non-monotonic: r(SQI, |error|) = **-0.033**, and MAE by SQI quartile runs 0.892, 0.796, 0.868, 0.769 - no trend. And there is a deeper problem: the only model with any skill is the demographics model, which uses no PPG at all, so 'signal quality predicting its error' has no mechanism and the small difference is most likely chance. **A quality index cannot be meaningfully validated against a model that ignores the signal it measures.**

### The dataset's own SNR claim could not be reproduced

The dataset reports 850 nm as cleanest (19.04 dB) and 940 nm as worst (16.44 dB, 17.5% of subjects below 10 dB). An independent computation - cardiac-band power including harmonics against out-of-band power - gives 8.1-8.3 dB for all four channels with **no meaningful ordering** and 71-73% of subjects below 10 dB.

The README does not define its SNR, so this is a difference of definition rather than a contradiction. It is reported rather than tuned into agreement: matching a published number by adjusting an undocumented definition would be fitting to the answer.

### The PPG arm: **NOT VIABLE** on this dataset

Stated plainly. Four-wavelength contact PPG, with the physically-motivated AC/DC and ratio-of-ratios features that pulse oximetry is built on, carries **no recoverable haemoglobin information** in these 252 subjects. It does not beat predicting the population mean, and it is beaten outright by asking the subject's sex.

The phone-deployability question is answered even more simply: **three of the four wavelengths are invisible to a phone camera**, so even a working four-wavelength method could not have transferred to the target hardware.

### What was tested, and what was not

* **Tested:** AC/DC per wavelength, ratio-of-ratios for all six wavelength pairs, perfusion index, pulse morphology summaries (beat CV, spectral purity, SNR, heart rate), linear (Ridge) and non-linear (gradient boosting) models, grouped 10-fold CV, against three baselines.
* **Not tested:** a deep model over raw waveforms. It is the remaining untried representation. It is recorded as untested, not as a proposed rescue.
* **Limits of this dataset:** one device, so the device-invariance half of the AC/DC claim is untestable; haemoglobin spans only 8.5-17.3 g/dL with SD 1.47 and **no severe anemia at all**, and a narrow range depresses every correlation.
